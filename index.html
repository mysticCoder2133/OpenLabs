What the bench is computing

The physics under the photons

Every pixel above is a number from these four ideas. Move a slider, then look here — the equation updates with the bench.

Why there are stripes

Each slit re-radiates the incoming wave (Huygens' principle). At a point on the screen the two waves arrive having travelled different distances. A whole number of wavelengths of path difference → they add, a bright fringe. Half a wavelength → they cancel, darkness.

The equation running this page

For small angles, adjacent bright fringes sit Δy apart on the screen. This is the whole experiment in one line — live, straight from the sliders:

Δy = λL / d
= (532 nm × 1.00 m) / 0.25 mm
= 2.13 mm ← fringe spacing right now I(θ) = I₀ · sinc²(πa·sinθ/λ) · [sin(Nα)/(N·sinα)]²
α = πd·sinθ/λ — single-slit envelope × N-slit interference

One photon at a time

Dim the source until one photon is in flight. Each lands as a single random dot — yet after thousands of dots the interference pattern appears. Each photon interferes with itself. Turn on the which-path detector and the fringes vanish: knowing the route destroys the superposition.

"The double-slit experiment contains the only mystery of quantum mechanics." — after Richard Feynman

Under the hood — no hand-waving

This is a real solver, not a canned animation:

  • field: steady-state Huygens sum Σ w·cos(kr−ωt), sinc envelope + obliquity per slit
  • screen: exact Fraunhofer I(θ) with sinθ = y/√(y²+L²) — no small-angle cheat
  • photons: inverse-CDF sampling of I(θ) → dots obey the Born rule
  • which-path: incoherent sum → sinc² envelope, fringes gone
  • ripple view is schematic; the detector readout is quantitative
From fascination to a real project

Should you start a research project? Yes — climb this ladder.

A fascination with one experiment is exactly how good research starts. The move is watching → simulating → measuring → publishing. This page is rung one.

RUNG 01

Ship the simulator

YOU ARE HEREthis week

You're looking at it. Own it: extend the bench with a polariser (three-polariser paradox), thin-film interference, or a photoelectric module. A physics engine you wrote and can explain line by line is genuine maker-portfolio material — MIT reads those.

→ output: live site + repo + a written "how the solver works" page
RUNG 02

Recreate Young on your desk

one weekend

Tape a single hair across a hole in cardboard — by Babinet's principle it diffracts like a slit of the same width. Shine a red laser pointer through it onto a wall 2–3 m away, photograph the fringes, measure Δy with a ruler, and solve λ = Δy·d/L. You should land near 650 nm. You have just measured the wavelength of light with a hair.

Class 2 pointer (<1 mW) only · never aim at eyes or mirrors
→ output: fringe photo + your λ estimate vs the diode's spec
RUNG 03

Make it quantitative

4–6 weeks

Swap the hair for wires of known gauge (different d), vary L across 20+ trials, and fit Δy against L/d with error bars. Compare measured λ to spec; quantify uncertainty from fringe-centroid pixel error. A linear fit with honest error analysis is what separates a demo from a study — and your simulator predicts every point you measure.

→ output: 8–10 page report · data, fits, residuals, error budget
RUNG 04

Go institutional

JC Year 5–6

Take the study to an A*STAR JC research attachment (RI can broker it) or submit through the Singapore Science & Engineering Fair — SSEF is an ISEF-affiliated pathway. A natural extension: single-photon statistics with an attenuated source, testing pattern build-up against your own Born-rule sampler. Pair it with SPhO training so the theory has depth behind it.

→ output: SSEF project / attachment report · mentor letter
RUNG 05

Package it for MIT

application season

MIT takes a research portfolio and a maker portfolio through SlideRoom. The triangle is simulator (you can build) + measurements (you can do science) + report (you can think). One coherent story about light, told three ways — that is a physics applicant worth admitting.

→ output: research portfolio + maker portfolio · one narrative
